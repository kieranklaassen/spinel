The parts of an interpolation run in order, each to its end

    r = R.new(%w[( a ) b])
    puts "#{r.item(r.nxt)} #{r.item(r.nxt)}"

printed "[)] a" for "[a] b" (R#item reads on from the queue after a
"("): in `"#{a.f(x1)} #{b.g(x2)}"` x1 and x2 ran first, then f, then
g, where CRuby runs x1, f, x2, g. interp_plan evaluates each part's
value in its place, inside the statement expression that builds the
String, but what a part's call hoists (the operands
emit_operands_in_order binds, a receiver's temp) went to the
statement's prelude, ahead of every part.

Once an earlier part has an effect, the hoists of a later part whose
operands hold a call or an assignment, or read a variable an earlier
part assigns, are kept in the part's place. They move only where the
interpolation is sequenced against the rest of its statement
(interp_sequenced): from the interpolation up to the body, each node
evaluates its children in order, or its other operands see nothing the
interpolation can change. Beside a call, an element read, a String or
a variable a part assigns, the C is as before: a read standing in the
same C expression could run before the moved operands, as in
`"#{k.name}#{k.two((n += 1), lg(1))}".center(n + 12, "*")`.
`buf << "..."` as a statement appends part by part and is sequenced.
